Research framework

Four steps from language to layout.

01

Cluster the language

Group repeated phrases into outcomes, objections, use contexts, and expectations instead of treating every review as an isolated anecdote. Keep the coding unit explicit: theme mention, review, or respondent.

02

Separate desire from proof

“Pain-free,” “gentle,” and “effective” are desired outcomes. Review frequency can prioritize a question; it cannot substantiate the answer.

03

Rank decision friction

Use count, severity, controllability, and relevance together. A lower-frequency irritation concern may require more caution than a higher-frequency packaging complaint.

04

Map each claim to a frame

Assign every gallery image or video one job: establish the product, demonstrate removal, clarify use, prove scale, or resolve an objection.

The creative brief

A gallery sequence built around confidence.

  1. 01 · Product clarity

    Lead with a clean pack presentation and immediately identify the product as individually packaged adhesive-remover wipes.

  2. 02 · Claim to proof

    Demonstrate removal with a realistic sequence. Avoid unsupported “instant” or absolute performance language.

  3. 03 · Skin experience

    Explain intended use while keeping irritation concerns and substantiation requirements visible to the team.

  4. 04 · Objection handling

    Use one frame to address residue, odor, and post-use comfort—the frictions customers mention after the main task.

  5. 05 · Relevant scenarios

    Prioritize medical tape, dressings, and device-related adhesive. Do not dilute the story with every edge-case use in the usage table.

  6. 06 · Expectation setting

    Show wipe dimensions, pack count, and usage steps clearly to reduce size and moisture surprises after delivery.

Research integrity

Review mining finds questions. It does not manufacture proof.

Customer language is excellent for identifying uncertainty and prioritizing communication. It cannot validate medical, safety, or performance claims by itself. The responsible workflow is to use coded themes to write the test plan, request substantiation, and shape the content hierarchy—then publish only what the product can prove.

Analysis walkthrough

How coded mentions become a defensible brief.

1. Define the unit before comparing counts

The archived tables count coded theme mentions, not unique reviewers. A review can express effectiveness, odor, residue, and ease of use at the same time, so theme counts can overlap. The denominator of 563 applies only to the rating-optimization theme table. Dividing 135 effective-removal mentions by 563 gives 23.98%; dividing 70 ineffective-removal mentions by the same table gives 12.43%. The separate negative-theme table has its own denominator, which is why 70 can also represent 24.91% there. Those percentages answer different within-table questions and must not be added.

2. Separate frequency, severity, and proof

Frequency helps prioritize what the content should address. Severity changes how cautiously the team must respond. Proof determines what can be claimed. For example, residue and odor are frequent post-use frictions that can shape an image or instruction. Irritation concerns may require more conservative language and substantiation even if their count is lower. Review mentions identify the shopper’s question; they do not verify a medical, safety, or performance answer.

3. Translate a tension into a communication job

“Effective removal” and “ineffective removal” are not contradictory errors in the research. Together they reveal a decision tension: shoppers want the outcome but do not uniformly experience it. The brief should therefore show the intended use and realistic process, clarify product size and format, and avoid absolute promises. Each creative frame needs one job so the sequence moves from recognition to proof, objection handling, and expectation setting.

4. Keep smaller tables directional

The buyer-motivation table contains 22 coded mentions, the expectation table 20, and the usage-scenario table 74. Those samples can suggest what to investigate or feature, but they do not estimate market preference. Professional recommendation appearing five times, for example, may be strategically interesting without being representative of all buyers. The correct next step is corroboration, not a universal claim.

5. Validate the brief before production

For every proposed headline or visual, trace the originating theme, state the intended shopper question, and identify the required substantiation. Confirm that dimensions, pack count, materials, instructions, and compatibility match the product record. Then test whether the frame clarifies the decision without overstating what the research can prove.

Repeatable next-step framework

Run every voice-of-customer brief through six gates.

  1. 01 · Scope

    Record source files, collection window if known, coding unit, table denominator, overlap rules, product scope, and material missing fields.

  2. 02 · Clean

    Normalize duplicate labels and obvious spelling variants while preserving source meaning. Do not silently merge themes with different intent.

  3. 03 · Rank

    Compare frequency with severity, controllability, commercial relevance, and confidence. Mark small samples as directional.

  4. 04 · Map

    Turn each priority tension into a shopper question, creative job, proposed evidence, and claim-risk note.

  5. 05 · Substantiate

    Request product specifications, test results, compliance review, or operational investigation before converting customer language into claims.

  6. 06 · Measure

    Define the intended outcome—clarity, click-through, conversion, lower returns, or fewer questions—and a guardrail against misleading expectations.

Practical decision rule: publish only the strongest statement supported by product evidence, even when customer language suggests a more dramatic promise.
